Photographing your
Catholic wedding.

Start with your parish

Every church has its own guidelines for photography, and they matter. Some parishes ask photographers to stay behind the altar rail or in the back; some allow movement only at certain points; many don’t allow flash during the liturgy.

What Jason does: before your wedding, he connects with your parish to understand their guidelines: where he can stand, when he can move, and whether flash is allowed. You shouldn’t have to manage that conversation.

What you can do: when you meet with your priest or wedding coordinator, mention who your photographer is and ask whether there’s a written policy to share.

The moments of the nuptial Mass

A wedding within Mass has a natural rhythm. Knowing it helps you picture the photographs you’ll treasure, and helps your photographer stay quiet and in the right place.

  1. The entrance procession. The walk down the aisle, and the faces waiting at the front.
  2. The Liturgy of the Word. Family members and friends proclaiming the readings you chose.
  3. The consent and vows. The heart of the sacrament. Your photographer should stay still and unobtrusive here.
  4. The blessing and exchange of rings. Close, quiet details worth planning a clear angle for.
  5. The Liturgy of the Eucharist. A time for reverence. Photographs are made sparingly and without distraction.
  6. The nuptial blessing. A beautiful, often-missed moment as the priest blesses you both.
  7. The recessional. Pure joy, and usually the most candid photographs of the day.

Why a second photographer helps

During a Mass, important moments happen at the altar and in the pews at the same time: your vows, and your parents’ faces as they hear them. One photographer who respects the liturgy can’t move freely to catch both.

A second photographer is especially helpful for larger guest counts, full Masses, or when you’d like both of you photographed getting ready in different places. They cover alternate angles and extra candids while Jason focuses on the main story.

Family photos without the stress

Even with a candid style, you’ll set aside dedicated time for family, wedding-party, and any special group photos you’d like. Many churches have a short window after Mass before the next service, so a written list helps:

  1. Write your list of groupings ahead of time, largest group first.
  2. Ask one organized relative from each side to gather people.
  3. Check with your parish how long you can stay in the sanctuary after the recessional.

Once the formals are done, Jason shifts back into a documentary approach so you can enjoy your people.

A calm timeline

  1. 9 to 18 months out: most couples book their photographer, especially for spring and fall dates.
  2. A few months out: your engagement session, a relaxed chance to get comfortable in front of the camera.
  3. Weeks before: Jason connects with your parish and you finalize together the day’s timeline and family list.
  4. The first week after: a small preview of your images.
  5. 6 to 8 weeks after: your full gallery, and the start of your album design if you choose one.

When plans change

Colorado weather has opinions. Part of the job is to stay calm and adaptable. If weather shifts or the schedule runs behind, Jason will help you adjust, suggest backup portrait locations (often inside your church, which can be the most beautiful option), and make sure we still capture what matters most.
